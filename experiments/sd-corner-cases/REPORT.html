Service Discovery Corner Cases

We ran six 87–100 node fleets on one machine for 35–55 minutes each. The goal was to find where kad-DHT service discovery slows down or breaks. Most of it held up. Three behaviours are broken and six are degraded.

The three to fix first
  1. A single libp2p_module bootstrap fills up. Nodes that join after it is full stay cut off for good, and every API call still reports success. 34 of 87 nodes were isolated.
  2. Registrations are rejected because the advertiser comes back 1–17 s late. The registrar allows only 1 s. On a loaded host about half of all retries miss it, and each miss restarts the wait from zero.
  3. The logoscore daemon segfaults when discovery calls run past their timeouts at 87–99 nodes. When the daemon crashes, the node goes down with it.

Interactive charts for every number here: SD corner cases dashboard. The source write-ups are in the harness repo under experiments/sd-corner-cases/ (PLAN.md, SUMMARY.md, reports/C1–C5.md).

How service discovery works

A node that offers a service, the advertiser, stores a small record called an ad on other nodes, the registrars. A node looking for the service, the looker, asks registrars for ads. Registrars do not accept an ad straight away. They hand out a ticket that says how long to wait first. The wait is longer when the registrar is busy, when the service already has many ads, or when the advertiser's IP looks like IPs it already holds. This is the spam protection.

The design leaves room for trouble in a few places: the 1 s retry window, the wait formula, the 10-minute maintenance round, and prune timing. We read the registrar and advertiser code first and wrote down eight predictions (P1–P8). Then we built one fleet case for each group of predictions. Their outcomes are at the end.

The setup

Everything ran on one laptop under docker (an OrbStack VM with 10 CPUs and 12.6 GB). No public fleet was used. Most nodes are a new lightweight node kind, sd-agent: a logoscore daemon that loads only libp2p_module. A small Python driver calls the module's public discovery API, the same calls delivery_module makes. Sd-agents only exercise discovery, so 100 of them fit on one host. A few real delivery-module nodes ran alongside as bystanders, to check that ordinary nodes still find each other.

The five cases

Each case asks one question that the code review raised. All cases share one bootstrap, cluster 42, one shard and service ids prefixed sd/. Runs are 35–55 minutes. That covers at least two advert lifetimes (15 min each) and two advertiser maintenance rounds (10 min each), so expiry and renewal effects show up.

C1 · Sharing a service with a big node

87 nodes · 45 min · run twice (C1, C1b)
Question
If one or two large “hub” nodes also advertise your service, does that make you slower to find? And how long does a hub with 43 services take to become visible?
Why
The registrar keeps a minimum wait per service and per IP. The code suggests one expensive registrant could raise the wait for everyone else on the same service (P2). It also suggests that a node's second and later services wait about 15 min each (P1).
Setup
2 hubs, each advertising 43 services. 40 “solo” nodes each advertise one of the hubs' services. 20 control nodes advertise services no hub touches. 4 late joiners start at +20 min. 10 lookers and 10 delivery bystanders.
Result
  • The first run hit a problem nobody predicted. The bootstrap stopped accepting connections at 50 inbound, and 34 of 87 nodes stayed isolated for the whole run (finding 1). We reran it as C1b with a bigger bootstrap. All later cases use that bootstrap.
  • Hubs do not slow down the sole advertiser of a shared service. Both groups were found in about 5–6 min (p90 8:52 vs 9:54).
  • The hubs themselves are slow. After 45 min only 30 of their 86 (hub, service) pairs were visible.
  • 4 of 4 lookers that made parallel calls crashed with a daemon segfault.

C2 · An advertiser that keeps stopping and starting

100 nodes · 45 min
Question
What happens to a “flapping” node that toggles its advert on and off, and to honest nodes that advertise the same service?
Why
A flapper comes back while its old ad is still cached, so its IP looks fully similar and it gets the maximum wait. Each flap may also raise the service's minimum wait for everyone (P3).
Setup
35 honest nodes on service a and 35 on control service b. 3 flappers on a: 5 s on / 5 s off, 30 s / 10 s and 120 s / 30 s. 3 + 3 late joiners at +12 min and again at +25 min. 8 lookers and 6 bystanders.
Result
  • Flappers disappear. The two fast ones were visible only until about 17 min. The 120 s one was never found in 1,334 lookups.
  • Late honest joiners of the flapped service took 5–13 min to be found. On the control service, four of six took 0:09–5:22. The sample is small: 3 nodes per cell.
  • A popular service is covered well. 43 of 44 providers were returned at some point. Flappers leaked no CPU or memory.

C3 · Growing the fleet, then starving it of CPU

30 → 99 nodes · 55 min
Question
How do lookups and registration change as the network grows? Does a slow, overloaded host make registrations fail?
Why
The ticket retry must arrive within 1 s, counted in whole seconds. Under CPU load a node may wake up late and miss it (P5).
Setup
Waves of 30, 20, 20 and 21 nodes join every 10 min. Each node advertises its own unique service and one shared service, sd/all. 8 lookers. From 40 to 48 min a separate container keeps all 10 CPUs busy.
Result
  • Lookups slow with size. The median went from 0.47 s at 39 nodes to 2.8 s at 99, and 6–16 % hit the module's 10 s call limit past about 70 nodes.
  • Rejections were 0 % up to about 60 nodes and more than half of all decisions at 99 nodes. They reached 76 % during the CPU burn.
  • The shared service gets slower to join as it gets more popular: 4:30 → 15:06. Unique services stay flat at about 7 min.
  • Two lookers crashed with the daemon segfault, even though they made one call at a time. One node never started.

C4 · Nodes leaving, a few and then many

100 nodes · 50 min
Question
How long does a node that has left stay findable? What happens when 30 % of the network disappears at once?
Why
Nothing tells registrars that a node stopped, and expired ads are pruned only every 15 min. The code suggests ghosts live 15–30 min (P6). Losing many registrars might also make lookups for rare services fail (P7).
Setup
50 nodes stay for the whole run. 15 stop at +12 min. 24 stop together at +25 min, which is 28 % of the nodes left. 10 lookers rotate over all services.
Result
  • Departed nodes were still returned 19–23 min after they left. The harness README says at most 15 min.
  • The mass drop cost about 5 minutes of extra failed lookups (21 % vs 13 %). Nodes that stayed remained 99–100 % findable.
  • After the drop, lookups got faster because the host had less to do.

C5 · Asking the registrar why it says no

87 nodes · 35 min · instrumented build
Question
What is the actual reason for the rejections seen in C1b and C3?
Why
The stock registrar logs the reason only at TRACE, so C1–C4 could only infer it from timing. This case turns the inference into a measurement.
Setup
A replay of C1b. libp2p_module was rebuilt from the same commit against nim-libp2p with a logging-only patch to registrar.nim. The patch logs each rejection reason, how late the retry was, and every decision with its wait and IP score. Behaviour is unchanged.
Result
  • All 1,632 rejections were ticket retries that arrived after the 1 s window, 1–17 s late (median 3 s).
  • 51 % of all ticket retries were rejected.
  • When a registrar already holds an ad from the same IP, the first wait is 957 s (median). Otherwise it is 319 s.
  • The clearest crash trace so far is from a hub. Its libp2p start took 89 s, it then fired 43 calls at once, they all overran, and the daemon segfaulted.

Findings

Ranked by impact on a real network. Severity reflects how bad the effect is and how easy it is to hit, not how hard it is to fix.

1 A single bootstrap fills up and silently isolates later joiners Critical

A libp2p_module bootstrap accepts at most maxInConnections incoming peers. That was 50 at the harness setting; the module's own default is 25. Kad keeps its connections open and nothing trims them, so the slots never free up. A node that knows only this bootstrap gets refused, never learns a second peer, and keeps redialling for the whole run. Its start, advertise and lookup calls all report success, and its lookups return an empty list in 12 ms.

52 connected 34 isolated
Who
12 of 40 solo nodes, 6 of 10 lookers, all 4 late joiners and 7 of 10 delivery nodes. The delivery nodes saw only 1–3 of their 10 peers.
Layer
libp2p_module bootstrap and connection limits (C1)
Fix ideas
Re-bootstrap from the peer store when the table stays empty. Report “no DHT peers” as an error or event. Trim idle kad connections on entry nodes. Document that more than one bootstrap is required.

2 Ticket retries arrive late and lose all their waiting High

The registrar accepts a retry only between issue time + W and issue time + W + 1 s, counted in whole seconds (registrar.nim:205-217). The advertiser sleeps exactly W after it receives the ticket. Nothing budgets for the network round trip, the truncated issue time or a busy event loop, so on a loaded host the retry is routinely late. A late retry is rejected. The advertiser drops the task (advertiser.nim:334), the slot stays empty until the next 10-minute maintenance round, and the next attempt starts without a ticket. That is why rejections arrive in waves 10 minutes apart.

Threshold
0 % rejected below about 60 nodes. 51–76 % at 87–99 nodes on a saturated host.
Layer
nim-libp2p registrar and advertiser (C1b, C3, C5)
Fix ideas
Widen registrationWindow. Let a late retry keep the wait it already served. Or have the advertiser aim early and retry inside the window.

3 The logoscore daemon segfaults after discovery calls time out High

The whole daemon dies (exit 139), and the node goes down with it. Every crash came after discovery calls ran past a deadline: libp2p_module's 10 s call budget, or logoscore's 20 s RPC deadline. Concurrent calls make it far more likely: 4 of 4 parallel lookers in C1b crashed, and so did a hub that fired 43 calls at once in C5. But two one-call-at-a-time lookers also crashed in C3. All crashes happened at 87–99 nodes with more than 800 % fleet CPU. C4 had the same timeouts and no crash, and a 47-node run did not reproduce it.

Hypothesis
A module reply that arrives after its RPC has already timed out reaches freed state in the daemon. Not proven: no core dump yet.
Layer
logoscore daemon (C1, C1b, C3, C5)
Next
Enable core dumps (ulimit -c plus compose ulimits) and replay the C5 hub pattern: concurrent calls into a module whose start is still in flight.

4 Each extra service from the same node waits about 15 minutes High

A registrar scores how similar your IP is to the IPs it already holds, across all services. Once a registrar accepts one ad from your node, your IP scores 1.0 there, and every further service from you waits about a full ad lifetime. A node offering many services therefore becomes visible one service at a time.

Compute IP similarity per service, or leave an IP's own existing ads out of its score.

5 A popular service gets slower to join the more popular it is Medium

Each registrar keeps a minimum wait per service. Every new registrant can push that minimum later, and the next newcomer must wait it out. With about 70 nodes already advertising sd/all, a new advertiser needed about 15 minutes. Each node's unique service, which nobody else advertises, stayed flat at about 7 minutes.

Layer
nim-libp2p registrar service lower bound (C3)

6–7 Flappers vanish and slow down honest joiners Medium Low

Penalising a node that toggles its advert is intended. The surprise is how complete the penalty is. stopAdvertising cancels any pending ticket (advertiser.nim:440), so a node whose off-switch comes sooner than its ticket wait never registers again. Its few early ads, accepted while caches were empty, age out after about 17 minutes. Honest nodes joining the same service later waited 5–13 minutes, against 0:09–5:22 for four of six on the control service.

8 A node that left stays findable for up to 30 minutes Medium

Nothing tells registrars that a node stopped, whether it stopped gracefully or through stopAdvertising. Expired ads are removed only when the registrar's prune loop runs, and that happens every 15 minutes (service_discovery.nim:74-77). An ad therefore lives between one and two lifetimes after the node's last registration. Clients should expect to dial dead records for a while after any departure.

Layer
nim-libp2p registrar prune loop (C4)

9–10 Lookups hit the 10 s call limit; a start can hang for good Medium Low

  • Lookup limit. A lookup for a rare service never collects 30 ads, so it walks every bucket. Past about 70 nodes on a saturated host, 6–20 % of these lookups failed at libp2p_module's 10 s synchronous call budget. All of them ended between 10.0 and 11.5 s.
  • Start hang. In a 100-node boot storm, one node in C3 and one in C4 never got their libp2p switch started within 180 s, and nothing retried. Starts that did finish took up to 89 s.
Layer
libp2p_module call budget and start (C1b, C3, C4, C5)

What held up

These behaved correctly and fast enough in every case where they were measured.

  • Finding a sole advertiserEvery one was found. Median 4–7 min, flat from 30 to 91 nodes.
  • Sharing a service with a hubNo slowdown against controls (p90 8:52 vs 9:54).
  • Coverage of a popular serviceLookups combine registrars up to 30 records; 43 of 44 providers were seen.
  • Late joiner into a formed networkFindable in 2:01–5:59 after joining at +20 min.
  • Delivery nodes behind a roomy bootstrap10 of 10 and 6 of 6 saw every /logos/delivery peer.
  • Losing 28 % of nodes at onceAbout 5 min of extra lookup failures; the rest stayed 99–100 % findable.
  • Renewal after one ad lifetimeNo flicker at 15 or 30 min; hit rates held at 93–100 %.
  • Flapper resource useNo leak: CPU, memory (38–46 MiB) and pending tasks matched honest nodes.
  • Registrar cache and memoryMedian 13 ads per registrar against a cap of 1,000. Module memory rose from 40 to 44 MiB in 45 min.

Caveats and where the data lives
  • One host. All nodes shared 10 CPUs. The host was saturated from about 60 nodes, so thresholds tied to load (findings 2, 3, 9, 10) will move on real, distributed hardware. The mechanisms behind them will not.
  • Bootstrap. After C1, the bootstrap had 400 connections (200 inbound), so findings 2–10 were measured on a fully connected fleet.
  • Lookers. From C2 on, lookers made one call at a time to avoid the crash.
  • Small samples. The flapper effect on joiners (finding 7) rests on 3 + 3 nodes per comparison.
  • Code. Harness branch experimental/sd-corner-cases, committed locally and not pushed. It adds the sd-agent kind, the driver, the analysis scripts and this experiment folder.
  • Instrumented build. The C5 logging patch is on the nim-libp2p branch experimental/sd-registrar-reasons, uncommitted and logging only.
  • Raw data. The traces, Prometheus dumps and logs for each run are under out/collect/sd-* on the test machine.
